# PNC

When I worked in health insurance at UPMC, the connection between medical anthropology, public health, and healthcare was pretty straightforward. The benefits of improving our healthcare system was immediately understandable. But that same intuitive feel is often lost when I try to link my previous work with the work I do now at PNC.

Additionally, with anyone who knows me from the"before times,"(as I've been calling doctoral program) and knew my scholarship or values, they look at me sideways now that I'm embedded within a bastion of neoliberalism in both form and function, by working at a national bank.

When I tell people that I work at PNC bank, they raise one eyebrow. When I tell them that I work on a human centered design team at that bank, they raise the other. And when I finally explain that I got to this point because of my doctoral training in Medical Anthropology and Public Health, their mouth tends to drop a little because none of it seems to make any sense. All that money and banking talk doesn't really add up for people (pun intended there).

I see and feel that there _is_ a tangible thread between the two arenas that is common to a number of industries, not just banking. Banking is just one arena in which these elements can converge. But when I try to explain that I do"ethnographic research at a bank,"people tend to ask me,"What the heck does ethnographic research look like at a bank?"

So, what I'm going to try to do here in this speech is to answer this question by explaining some of the synergies I see and feel between the banking work that I currently do, and the field and discipline of anthropology that I've always loved.

Where I see these connections clearly is through the lens of design, and the principles, elements, or logics that inform the design process; whether it be to design something material like a product, or metaphysical in nature like a system or service.

I'll use a more concrete example from my dissertation research concerning infrastructural design to illustrate how anthropology's knowledge and methods can allow us to deeply investigate and understand the logics that inform and guide design decisions. This investigation, when applied in spaces like the public and private sectors, can enable us to build better systems, services, and even products that tangibly enhance people's lived experiences.

## The Anthropological Turn

As material objects, infrastructures elicit thoughts of the physical systems through which other matter is enabled or hindered to move. Brian Larkin illuminates only a subset of their functions by describing infrastructures as,"the physical networks through which goods, ideas, waste, power, people, and finance are trafficked."Recent scholarship has advanced conceptions of infrastructures beyond their materiality to also encompass the assemblages of actants and techniques of praxis which give rise to their form as well.

Nikhil Anand's work on Mumbai's water infrastructure describes the city as a"social-material assemblage, an unstable process of relations between social imaginaries, institutions, human bodies, and things that are always in formation and always falling apart."He describes how,"[t]his infrastructure is a living, breathing, leaking assemblage of more than human relations. It is composed as much of steel and cement as'nature,'laws, social histories, and political practices."The social forces that create and, in turn, are then created by infrastructures makes the relationship between their technical and social attributes entangled and interdependent.

An infrastructure's form does not only reflect the task it emerged to solve, but also the social logics guiding its design. The arrangement, or form, of these infrastructures can point to the implicit, yet often overlooked, cultural constructs that are embedded within them, and that can produce harmful effects and disparities for those who are either forced to use these systems, or left unconnected and without access to them.

Scholars Rogers and O'Neill describe how,"[i]nfrastructure is a key factor shaping people's direct relationships both with each other and with their environment in cities. It de-mar-cates both literally and figuratively which points in urban contexts can and should be connected, and which should not, the kinds of people and goods that can and should circulate easily, and which should stay put, and who can and should be integrated within the city, and who should be left outside of it."

Despite their so-called"invisibility,"Rogers and O'Neill explain how infrastructural systems provide"an ideal ethnographic site for theorizing how broad and abstract social orderings such as the state, citizenship, criminality, ethnicity and class play out concretely at the level of everyday practice, revealing how such relationships of power and hierarchy translate into palpable forms of physical and emotional harm."

Investigating infrastructures as vectors of social logics can help forefront how forms of political rationality underlie these projects, yet the infrastructural violence they create is typically left unaddressed by those in power who possess the ability to redesign these systems, but simply lack the desire or political will.

Wendell Berry describes the biopolitics of the global states system in a similar way, noting that,"[t]hese inequalities and exploitations, are not flaws in the system, they are inherent in the system."When infrastructures are reconceptualized as reflecting of social logics, any lacks and breakdowns must also be reconceptualized as consciously built and innate features of the system itself--not as an anomaly resulting from some critical event over which governing entities had little or no control.

So, when a neighborhood doesn't have a bus line that runs through it, or when a train derails and poisons a community, or when Flint Michigan's water is still toxic after all of these years, these events are not merely a matter of unfortunate circumstance. They are in fact, the result of a particular"technopolitics of life,"put forth by those in power that shapes the design and implementation of these infrastructural systems that directly affect the lives and embodied experiences of individuals and communities.

## Conclusion

Infrastructural design, systems design, product design. All of these types of work are sociotechnical and imbued with power, logics of sociopolitical decision-making, and processes of cultural meaning making in ways that are not always apparent because they are taken for granted, or seen as natural, and eventually blend in with the layered fabrics of society, like our postal system, or the width of a door frame. Even the layout of streets in a city were all _decisions_ that were made. People don't just build roads to nowhere.

All of these facets of our everyday world were at one point design decisions that were made with social constructs, or set of logics, that guided their creation or implementation. There is intention in our sociotechnical systems, services, and materials that must be taken into account as we engage with and investigate them.

Anthropologists and Anthropology as a discipline, contain a unique sensibility that we can bring into the public and private sectors where we conduct similar research. Anthropological methods and the discipline's rich body of theory and knowledge enables us to recognize, investigate, and critique these intentions in ways that other disciplines often struggle with.

Anthropology has a wealth of tools that help us to recognize and reveal these constructs for exactly what they are… socially constructed. By people. With intention and purpose.

Anthropology enables us to then problematize them explicitly and describe what factors play into not only the power dynamics laden within these sociotechnical systems, but also the impact they have on people's lived experiences when they actually encounter the frictions of daily life.

And so, I see anthropology, its theories, methodologies, and core tenants of cultural relativism and participant observation as an invaluable skill set, that when brought into other spaces like in the public sector (in the case of my thesis research) or the private sector (as with my current position at PNC), uncover and reveal things that other disciplines aren't equipped to investigate or even take note of in some instances.

That perspective and ability to look at a system, service, or product, and trace it's elements back to the factors and logics that played into it's design, and to understand how that design will actually affect people's lived experiences (for better or worse), that is the crux of my work today. I aim to bring that human-centered perspective into everything that we build at the bank. And I use anthropological thinking, theory, and methods every single day to continue to keep that perspective and bring it into the work that we do every day.

In a place like a bank where you have a bunch of data and number-oriented people, we relentlessly bring the human element into the mix and are constantly making product managers, UX and UI designers, or executives consider the lived experience of real individuals and understand the factors and facets of their experience at a much deeper level than numbers alone can describe. We ultimately want to help design our systems, services, or products to not only be more enjoyable for people, but also hopefully, truly meaningful and ultimately beneficial to their everyday lives.

So, instead of supporting detrimental design logics like that of profit, or productivity, or some other detached and harmful goal, when I am involved with a project, my job is to put the human experience and anthropological perspective at the nexus of the work. Because of that charter, all of the projects I have been on thus far have directly benefitted from the perspective, experience, and skill set that I've gained through my anthropological training, which all started here at Tufts 12 years ago and which I am glad to be able talk about with you all today.
